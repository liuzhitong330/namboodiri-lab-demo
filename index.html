Mesolimbic Dopamine and the Geometry of Learning: When Does Duration Matter?

Cathy Liu · cathyliu014@gmail.com · personal exploratory project, July 2026
fiber photometry dopamine associative learning prediction error inter-reward interval Python / NWB

The Namboodiri lab studies how the brain learns — specifically, how mesolimbic dopamine encodes causal associations and how the structure of experience (not just its content) shapes learning rates. Their work combines fiber photometry, head-fixed behavior, and optogenetics to dissect the circuit-level mechanisms behind reinforcement learning in mice.

Their 2022 paper (Jeong et al., Nature Neuroscience) showed that dopamine signals in the nucleus accumbens convey causal associations — not just reward prediction errors, but information about which stimuli reliably precede rewards. I wanted to look at the raw photometry data underlying those claims, and to connect it to the lab's more recent 2026 finding that changed how I think about learning rate.

What this is. Personal exploratory analysis, not novel research. Fiber photometry data downloaded from DANDI archive 000351 (Jeong et al. 2022, "Mesolimbic dopamine release conveys causal associations"). Session: subject HJ-FP-F1, Day 12 (fully learned). GCaMP6f signal in NAc, 120 Hz sampling, 50 cue trials and 100 reward trials extracted. Per-trial baseline subtraction (pre-event window). Schematic in Figure 2 is illustrative — it depicts the conceptual finding of Namboodiri et al. 2026 (Nat Neurosci), not a re-analysis of their data.

Dopamine responds to both cue and reward in a learned association

The cue response is the key signature of a learned association. A naive animal would show dopamine responses only to reward (the unconditioned stimulus). By Day 12, the cue alone drives a dopamine rise — evidence that the mesolimbic circuit has encoded the cue→reward contingency. The reward response remains, but in a fully-learned temporal difference framework, cue responses should eventually equal or exceed reward responses as the animal "pre-pays" its prediction error at the earliest reliable predictor.

What controls how fast this learning happens?

Duration between rewards, not trial count, controls learning rate. The intuitive assumption has always been that learning rate scales with the number of reward pairings — that's how most RL models are parameterized, and it's what decades of animal conditioning experiments seemed to show. Namboodiri et al. 2026 showed this is wrong: the inter-reward interval is the primary determinant of how fast an association forms. An animal given 20 rewards 90 seconds apart learns faster than one given 20 rewards 5 seconds apart, even though the total reward count is identical. The implication is that the mesolimbic system is tracking elapsed time, not event counts — which raises questions about the neural implementation that current dopamine/TD models do not clearly answer.
The connection to the 2022 data is direct. The photometry traces in Figure 1 show dopamine dynamics in a session where the IRI was fixed. What the 2026 paper asks is: how would these cue-response magnitudes differ if the IRI had been shorter? The prediction is that cue responses (the "learned" component) would be smaller — not because fewer trials occurred, but because the brain's internal timer generates weaker associations when rewards arrive in rapid succession. This is a testable prediction in the existing DANDI datasets (sessions with varying ITIs) that I found interesting to think about.

Connection to the lab's work

  • Temporal structure of learning: The IRI finding raises a specific mechanistic question: which neurons encode elapsed time between rewards, and how does that signal interact with dopamine release? One possibility is that the VTA tracks reward history with adaptation-like dynamics — slow IRI → fuller recovery → larger dopaminergic response to next reward. Testing this would require photometry in VTA alongside NAc during IRI manipulation experiments, which I'd be excited to contribute to.
  • Causal associations vs. mere correlations: The 2022 Jeong et al. paper showed that dopamine specifically encodes causal cue→reward relationships rather than temporal correlations. I'm interested in how the lab operationalizes "causal" in the context of the IRI finding — if the animal assigns higher causal weight to a reward that follows a longer delay, that has implications for how dopamine circuits represent cause-and-effect that go beyond standard TD models.
  • On the experimental side, I have experience with mammalian cell culture, Western blot, ELISA, flow cytometry, fluorescence microscopy, and protein analytical methods. I'm also comfortable with Python-based data analysis, NWB/DANDI data formats, and have worked with time-series neural data computationally. I'd be glad to contribute across bench and analysis.

Methods

Fiber photometry data from DANDI archive 000351 (Jeong et al. 2022, Nat Neurosci). Subject HJ-FP-F1, session Day12-learned. Raw NWB file read with h5py; GCaMP signal extracted from acquisition/photometry/GCaMP (120 Hz). Peri-event traces: −2 s to +5 s window around each event onset; per-trial baseline subtraction using the mean of the pre-event window (first 2 s). Event timestamps from processing/behavior/BehavioralEpochs. Cue = Sound1 (n=50 trials); Reward = Fixed solenoid 3 (n=100 trials, including uncued). Figure 2 is a schematic based on the conceptual result reported in Namboodiri et al. 2026, Nat Neurosci (DOI forthcoming); curves are illustrative only. Figures: pure Python SVG. Code: fetch_data.py, make_figures.py. Trace data: traces_summary.tsv.
